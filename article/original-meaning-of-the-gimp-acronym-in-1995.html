Original Meaning of the GIMP Acronym in 1995

Today, GIMP is globally recognized as one of the most powerful free and open-source raster graphics editors available. While modern users know it as the GNU Image Manipulation Program, the software carried a slightly different name during its inception. This article explains the original expansion of the GIMP acronym upon its public release in 1995 and details why the name subsequently changed.

When the software was first announced to the public on November 21, 1995, the acronym GIMP stood for General Image Manipulation Program.

The project originated at the University of California, Berkeley, created by computer science students Spencer Kimball and Peter Mattis as a semester-long class assignment. In their initial release announcement posted to the Usenet newsgroup comp.os.linux.development.apps, the developers introduced the software as the "General Image Manipulation Program," designed to provide Unix and Linux systems with an extensible, motif-based photo editing alternative to proprietary software like Adobe Photoshop.

The transition to its current name occurred a year later, in 1996. Kimball and Mattis met with Richard Stallman, the founder of the Free Software Foundation (FSF) and the GNU Project. Stallman asked permission to adopt the program into the GNU software ecosystem. The creators agreed, and the "G" in GIMP was officially changed from "General" to "GNU," resulting in the name it holds today: the GNU Image Manipulation Program.
