Circular Motion Blur for Wheels in GIMP

This article explains how GIMP's GEGL Circular Motion Blur filter creates realistic, high-speed rotational movement on vehicle wheels. You will learn the mechanics behind the filter, how it calculates radial blurring around a fixed center point, and the exact steps required to isolate, configure, and blend the effect for an authentic spinning appearance.

The Mechanics of Circular Motion Blur

The GEGL Circular Motion Blur filter simulates rotation by taking pixels within an image and sweeping them along concentric circular paths. Instead of displacing pixels along a straight vector like a linear motion blur, the circular algorithm requires two primary coordinates: a center point (X and Y axis) and a rotation angle.

During rendering, the filter samples pixels along an arc defined by the chosen angle, averaging their color and luminance values. Areas farthest from the center travel a greater physical distance along the arc, naturally producing a more pronounced, elongated blur toward the outer rim and tire tread. Conversely, pixels near the hub experience minimal displacement, accurately replicating real-world rotational physics captured by a camera's shutter.

Preparing the Wheel

To achieve a convincing result, the wheel must be isolated from the rest of the vehicle. Applying the filter globally will distort the wheel arches, bodywork, and ground.

  1. Use the Ellipse Select Tool or Paths Tool to create a precise selection of the wheel rim and tire.
  2. Exclude non-rotating components, such as brake calipers and visible suspension elements, by subtracting them from the selection.
  3. Duplicate the selection onto a new layer so the blur can be adjusted non-destructively without altering the background.

Setting the Center Point and Angle

  1. With the isolated wheel layer active, navigate to Filters > Blur > Circular Motion Blur.
  2. Identify the wheel's physical center (usually the center cap or axle nut). In the filter settings, adjust the Center X and Center Y parameters until the crosshair sits directly on this focal point. Incorrect center alignment causes an off-axis wobble that breaks the illusion of rotation.
  3. Adjust the Angle slider to dictate the perceived speed. A small angle (between 1° and 5°) produces a subtle roll suitable for low speeds. Higher values (10° to 25° or more) generate intense streaks that suggest highway or track speeds.

Refining Static Elements

In real-world photography, brake calipers and rotors do not rotate with the wheel. To finalize the effect:

  1. Place the original unblurred image layer beneath your blurred wheel layer.
  2. Add a Layer Mask to the blurred wheel layer.
  3. Using a soft black brush on the mask, paint away the blurred areas directly over the brake caliper so the sharp, stationary caliper from the bottom layer shows through.
  4. Soften the outer edge of the tire using a slight mask feather to seamlessly integrate the spinning tread with the road surface and wheel well shadow.
