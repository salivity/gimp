GEGL Zoom Motion Blur in GIMP Explained

The GEGL Zoom Motion Blur filter in GIMP creates a dynamic visual effect simulating high-speed movement toward or away from a focal point. This article explains the underlying mechanism of the filter, detailing how it manipulates pixels along radial vectors to trick the human eye into perceiving rapid forward motion, commonly known as a "warp" or "rushing" effect.

The Mechanism of Radial Scaling

At its core, the Zoom Motion Blur filter works through radial interpolation. Unlike standard linear motion blur, which smears pixels along a flat, one-dimensional axis (such as horizontally or vertically), zoom motion blur operates radially from an origin point.

  1. Defining the Center Point: The user or default setting defines a specific coordinate \((X, Y)\) on the image canvas as the anchor. This point serves as the vanishing point of the motion.
  2. Radial Sampling: The algorithm calculates straight lines (vectors) extending outward from this central coordinate to every pixel on the canvas.
  3. Iterative Scaling and Blending: The filter generates multiple semi-transparent copies of the image at progressively varying scale levels. It scales the image outward (or inward) relative to the center anchor, then blends these overlapping iterations together.

Creating the Illusion of Depth and Speed

The sensation of "rushing toward the center" relies on optical physics and visual perception:

  • Peripheral Stretching: Because scale expansion increases proportionally with distance from the center point, pixels near the edges of the frame travel much farther during the scaling process than pixels near the middle. This creates long, pronounced streaks along the perimeter while leaving the exact center relatively sharp.
  • Simulating Optical Zoom Bursts: In traditional photography, this effect is achieved by adjusting the focal length of a zoom lens while the camera's shutter is open. The GEGL filter replicates this "zoom burst" digitally by averaging the pixel values along the path of magnification.
  • Perceptual Convergence: The human brain interprets converging linear streaks as perspective lines receding into three-dimensional space. When the edges are heavily blurred along these perspective lines and the center remains comparatively legible, the visual system automatically decodes the image as rapid acceleration toward the clear focal point.
