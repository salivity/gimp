Linear vs Perceptual Gamma in GIMP Explained

In GIMP, choosing between Linear and Perceptual gamma modes dictates how color and brightness values are mathematically calculated during editing. Perceptual gamma matches how human vision interprets brightness, making it ideal for standard visual adjustments, while Linear gamma calculates light based on physical, real-world intensity. Understanding the distinction between these modes is essential for achieving accurate color blending, realistic lighting effects, and proper image scaling.

Understanding the Core Concepts

  • Perceptual Gamma (Non-Linear/sRGB): Human eyes are naturally more sensitive to subtle differences in dark tones than in bright highlights. Perceptual gamma applies a curve (traditionally the sRGB gamma curve) that allocates more data to shadow details so images look balanced to human vision on standard computer displays.
  • Linear Gamma (Linear Light): In physics, doubling the amount of light doubles the energy. Linear gamma processes color values using direct, proportional mathematics where zero is black, one is white, and intermediate values represent the exact physical amount of light without any perceptual correction curve.

Key Practical Differences in GIMP

1. Color Blending and Gradients

  • Perceptual: Blending two high-contrast colors (such as red and green) often produces a muddy, dark band in the middle.
  • Linear: Generates smooth, physically accurate transitions. The colors mix as real light rays would, eliminating unnatural dark fringes between complementary colors.

2. Blurring and Filters

  • Perceptual: Applying a Gaussian blur to bright elements on a dark background can cause the bright areas to dim significantly because the non-linear math underestimates the strength of the light.
  • Linear: Retains the true intensity of highlights when blurred, resulting in natural-looking glows, bokeh effects, and realistic light diffusion.

3. Image Scaling and Resampling

  • Perceptual: Downscaling an image with fine, high-contrast details (like black-and-white patterns or sharp text) often darkens the overall image.
  • Linear: Preserves the correct average luminance when resizing, preventing unwanted shifts in exposure.

4. Layer Modes and Compositing

  • Perceptual: Layer blending modes (such as Multiply, Screen, or Overlay) produce the classic behavior found in older 8-bit digital imaging software.
  • Linear: Compositing calculates light addition and subtraction realistically, which is standard practice in professional visual effects (VFX) and 3D rendering pipelines.

When to Use Each Mode

  • Use Linear Mode for: Resizing images, applying blurs, compositing lighting effects, rendering 3D textures, and creating smooth color gradients.
  • Use Perceptual Mode for: Final tone curve adjustments, traditional digital painting where brush behavior is expected to mimic classic software, and legacy workflows requiring exact visual matching to older sRGB-only projects.
