Linear Burn Blend Mode in GIMP Explained

The Linear Burn blend mode in GIMP is a dark composite mode designed to significantly darken images and intensify contrast by summing pixel values and subtracting brightness. This article provides a comprehensive breakdown of the mathematical function behind Linear Burn, its visual characteristics compared to other darken modes, and its primary applications in digital image editing and compositing.

How Linear Burn Works

Linear Burn belongs to the "Darken" family of blend modes in GIMP. It evaluates the color information in both the active (upper) layer and the base (lower) layer, decreasing the brightness of the base layer based on the darkness of the blend layer.

Mathematically, Linear Burn adds the pixel values of the blend layer and the base layer together and then subtracts the maximum value (1.0 in normalized float values, or 255 in 8-bit color depth):

\[\text{Result} = \text{Base} + \text{Blend} - 1.0\]

If the resulting value is less than zero, GIMP clips it directly to pure black (\(0\)).

Key Characteristics of Linear Burn

  • Aggressive Darkening: Because it uses a linear subtraction method rather than a multiplicative curve, Linear Burn darkens images faster and more aggressively than the Multiply blend mode.
  • Shadow Clipping: Shadow tones quickly clip to pure black wherever the combined values of the base and blend layers are low.
  • Neutral Color: Pure white (\(255, 255, 255\)) acts as the neutral color. Blending a white layer using Linear Burn results in no change to the underlying image. Pure black on the blend layer forces the base image directly to pure black.
  • Color Saturation: In areas where colors do not clip to black, Linear Burn produces highly saturated midtones and deep, rich shadow transitions.

Linear Burn vs. Multiply and Color Burn

While all three modes belong to the Darken category, their mathematical operations produce distinct visual results:

  • Multiply: Multiplies pixel values together. It preserves shadow details better and produces a natural darkening effect without harsh clipping.
  • Color Burn: Increases contrast by dividing the inverted base layer by the blend layer. It produces intense midtone saturation and high contrast, but leaves highlights relatively intact.
  • Linear Burn: Decreases overall brightness uniformly through linear subtraction. It clips shadows much earlier than Multiply and produces darker overall results than Color Burn without the extreme midtone contrast boost.

Practical Applications in GIMP

  1. Texture Integration: Applying grungy or paper textures to artwork where deep, stark shadows are needed.
  2. Dynamic Shading: Creating harsh, dramatic lighting effects by painting with desaturated or complementary colors on a separate layer.
  3. Ink and Line Art Blending: Merging scanned line art onto colored backgrounds while ensuring the ink remains deep and solid.
  4. Vignetting and Light Leaks: Creating dark, moody edge vignettes to draw focus toward the center of a composition.
