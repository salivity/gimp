What Does XCF Stand for in GIMP?

In the context of GIMP (GNU Image Manipulation Program) development history, the acronym XCF stands for eXperimental Computing Facility. This article explains the origins of the XCF name, its historical connection to the University of California, Berkeley, and the role of the XCF file format as the native project container for GIMP.

The Origin of the Name

The acronym traces directly back to the student computing group where GIMP was first conceived: the eXperimental Computing Facility (XCF) at the University of California, Berkeley.

In 1995, two UC Berkeley undergraduate students and XCF members, Spencer Kimball and Peter Mattis, set out to develop a free, open-source alternative to proprietary image editors like Adobe Photoshop. As a tribute to the undergraduate computer club that provided the resources, environment, and community for their work, they named their software's native save format after the group.

What the eXperimental Computing Facility Was

Founded in the mid-1970s, the eXperimental Computing Facility was an official student organization at UC Berkeley dedicated to providing advanced Unix computing environments to undergraduates. The group served as an incubator for major open-source developments and innovative programming projects throughout the 1980s and 1990s. Kimball and Mattis developed both the initial versions of GIMP and the GTK (GIMP Toolkit) framework on the systems housed inside the facility.

The Role of XCF in GIMP

Just as .psd is the native working format for Adobe Photoshop, .xcf is the native file extension for GIMP.

Unlike standard export formats such as JPEG or PNG, which flatten an image into a single graphical layer, the XCF format preserves all editing data. It stores:

  • Individual layers and their blending modes
  • Layer masks and alpha transparency channels
  • Vector paths and guides
  • Text layers and font metadata
  • Selections and color profiles

Using the XCF format allows creators to pause their work, close the application, and reopen the project later with the complete non-destructive editing history and layer structure intact. Today, the format remains a direct historical link to the Berkeley student lab where modern open-source graphics editing began.
